Credit

How credit scores work and how to raise yours

A three-digit number can change the price of a mortgage by tens of thousands of dollars. The good news: the rules behind it are public, and most of them are in your control.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20268-minute read

What a credit score measures

A credit score predicts how likely you are to fall 90 days behind on a payment in the next two years. Lenders use it to decide whether to approve you and at what rate; landlords, insurers and some employers may look at credit reports too. The most widely used models in the US are FICO and VantageScore, both on a 300–850 scale. Canada's bureaus use a similar 300–900 range.

The five factors, by weight

FICO publishes the approximate weight of each category for the general population:

FactorWeightWhat helps
Payment history35%Every payment on time, every month
Amounts owed (utilization)30%Card balances low relative to limits
Length of credit history15%Older accounts kept open
New credit10%Few recent applications
Credit mix10%A mix of cards and installment loans over time

Two factors, payment history and utilization, drive about two-thirds of the score. That's where to focus.

What counts as a good score?

On the FICO scale, roughly 670–739 is considered good, 740–799 very good and 800+ exceptional. Lenders set their own tiers, but crossing 740 often unlocks the best mortgage and auto pricing. Below about 580, approval gets hard and rates get expensive.

How to raise your score, in order of impact

  1. Never miss a payment again. Set every account to autopay at least the minimum. A payment 30 or more days late can be reported and stays on your report for up to seven years, though its impact fades over time.
  2. Cut utilization. Keep card balances below 30% of each limit and overall; people with the highest scores tend to stay under 10%. Paying the card before the statement date lowers the balance that gets reported.
  3. Fix errors. Pull your reports (free weekly in the US at AnnualCreditReport.com) and dispute anything wrong with the bureau that shows it.
  4. Keep old cards open, especially ones with no annual fee. Closing them shortens your history and cuts available credit.
  5. Space out applications. Each hard inquiry can shave a few points and stays visible for two years. Rate-shopping for a mortgage or auto loan within a short window is usually treated as one inquiry.
  6. Building from zero? A secured card or a credit-builder loan, used lightly and paid in full, creates history in six months or so.

Key takeaway: On-time payments and low balances do most of the work. Everything else is fine-tuning.

What a better score is worth

On a $360,000, 30-year mortgage, a rate one percentage point lower (say 6.5% instead of 7.5%) cuts the monthly payment by about $240 and total interest by about $87,000. Try it yourself in the mortgage calculator.

Myths to ignore

  • Checking your own score hurts it. No, that's a soft inquiry.
  • You need to carry a balance. No. Paying in full each month builds the same history with zero interest.
  • Income affects your score. Not directly; income isn't in your credit report.
