Emergency fund: how much you need and where to keep it

An emergency fund turns a crisis into an inconvenience. It's the first thing to build and the last thing to raid.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20267-minute read

What an emergency fund is for

An emergency fund is cash set aside for expenses that are both necessary and unexpected: a job loss, a medical bill, an urgent car or home repair, emergency travel. It is not for holidays, a new phone or a sale, all of which you can plan for in your regular budget.

Without one, emergencies land on a credit card at 20–30% interest, or force you to sell investments at the worst possible time. Survey data from the US Federal Reserve has repeatedly shown that a large share of adults would struggle to cover even a few hundred dollars of unexpected expense with cash. That gap is what this fund closes.

How much is enough?

The standard guidance is three to six months of essential expenses: the needs in your budget, not your full income. If your essentials are $3,000 a month, that's $9,000 to $18,000.

Aim nearer three months if

  • You have a stable job in a field that hires quickly
  • Your household has two independent incomes
  • You have no dependents and low fixed costs

Aim for six months or more if

  • You're self-employed, on commission or a contract worker
  • You're the only earner, or you support children or parents
  • You own a home or an older car
  • You work in a cyclical industry that cuts jobs in recessions

How to build it without stalling

  1. Start with a $1,000 or one-month mini-fund. It covers most small surprises and builds the habit.
  2. Automate a transfer every payday, even $50. Use the savings goal calculator to set the amount that hits your target by a date.
  3. Redirect windfalls. Tax refunds, bonuses and cash gifts can do months of work in one go.
  4. Balance it with debt payoff. If you carry high-interest debt, get the mini-fund in place first, then attack the debt, then finish the full fund.

Where to keep it

The fund needs three properties, in this order: safe, accessible, and earning something.

OptionSafetyAccessBest for
High-yield savings accountDeposit-insured1–2 daysThe core of the fund
Money market accountDeposit-insured at banks1–2 daysSame as above, sometimes with cheques
Short-term CDs or T-billsVery highLocked until maturityThe "month 4 to 6" layer
Stocks or cryptoCan fall 30% or moreFast, but at any priceNot an emergency fund

In the US, FDIC insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category; in Canada, CDIC covers up to $100,000 per eligible category. Keep the fund at a different bank from your everyday account if you're tempted to dip into it.

Key takeaway: The goal isn't high returns. It's having the money, in full, on the worst day of your year.

When to use it, and how to refill it

Ask three questions: Is it necessary? Is it urgent? Is it unexpected? If all three answers are yes, use the fund without guilt; that's what it's for. Then pause extra investing and refill it first, using the same automatic transfer that built it.

Sources

  • Federal Reserve, Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking
  • FDIC, deposit insurance
  • Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation
