Index funds and ETFs: a beginner's guide

You don't need to pick winning stocks. Owning the whole market, cheaply, has beaten most professionals over long periods.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20268-minute read

What they are

An index fund holds every security in a market index, such as the S&P 500, the total US stock market or a global index, in roughly the same proportions. It doesn't try to beat the market; it tries to be the market, at the lowest cost possible.

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund that trades on a stock exchange like a share. Many ETFs track indexes, so "index ETF" is common. Traditional index mutual funds are bought from the fund company at the end-of-day price. For most long-term investors the difference is minor: both can be excellent.

Why they work

Low cost

Broad index funds often charge 0.03%–0.20% a year. Many actively managed funds charge 0.5%–1% or more. Costs are certain; outperformance isn't. S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA scorecards have consistently found that most actively managed US large-cap funds trail the S&P 500 over 10- and 15-year periods.

Instant diversification

One total-market fund can hold thousands of companies. A single company's collapse barely registers, unlike a portfolio of five stocks.

Tax efficiency and simplicity

Low turnover means fewer taxable distributions in regular accounts, and a three-fund portfolio is easy to understand, rebalance and stick with.

How to compare two funds

  • What index it tracks: total market, S&P 500, international, bonds?
  • Expense ratio: the yearly fee. Lower is better when two funds track the same index.
  • Tracking difference: how closely the fund has matched its index after costs.
  • Size and trading volume: larger, heavily traded ETFs have tighter bid-ask spreads.
  • Account fit: some funds are cheaper or only available at certain brokers.

A simple portfolio

A common starting point is a "three-fund" mix: a domestic stock index fund, an international stock index fund and a bond index fund. The split depends on your time horizon and how you'd react to a 30% drop:

HorizonStocksBonds
20+ years80–100%0–20%
10–20 years60–80%20–40%
Under 10 years30–60%40–70%

Prefer one decision? Target-date funds hold a ready-made mix that gets more conservative as the year in the fund's name approaches. These are illustrations, not recommendations. Your mix should reflect your own goals and risk tolerance.

Key takeaway: Choose a sensible mix, keep costs low, automate contributions, rebalance once a year, and don't sell in a panic.

How to start this week

  1. Pick the account: an employer plan first if there's a match, then an IRA or Roth IRA (US), RRSP or TFSA (Canada), ISA (UK), or the equivalent where you live.
  2. Open it at a low-cost broker or fund company.
  3. Buy your chosen funds and set up an automatic monthly contribution.
  4. Check what it could grow to with the compound interest calculator.

Risks to understand

Index funds fall when markets fall, sometimes by 30–50% in a severe bear market. They don't protect you from the market itself, only from picking the wrong slice of it. Money needed within about five years belongs in safer assets.
