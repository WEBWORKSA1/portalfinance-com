Home buying

How much house can you afford?

The bank will tell you the most you can borrow. That's a ceiling, not a target. Here's how to find a price that still lets you save, travel and sleep at night.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20268-minute read

How lenders decide

Most lenders look at your debt-to-income ratios, your credit score, your down payment and your savings after closing.

  • Front-end ratio: housing costs (principal, interest, tax, insurance, PMI, HOA) ÷ gross monthly income. A classic guideline is 28% or less.
  • Back-end ratio: housing plus all other debt payments ÷ gross income. Conventional guidance is about 36%; many programs approve up to 43% or higher with strong compensating factors.

On a $120,000 household income ($10,000 a month gross), 28% means about $2,800 a month for all housing costs.

A stricter test that protects your future

Lender ratios use gross income and ignore your goals. A healthier check uses take-home pay: keep total housing costs at or below about 30–35% of net income, after you've set aside at least 15% for retirement and kept your emergency fund intact. If the house only works by pausing retirement savings, it's too expensive.

Worked example

A couple takes home $7,600 a month on $120,000 gross. Applying 33% of take-home gives a housing budget of about $2,500 a month. With 10% down, a 6.5% 30-year fixed rate, 1.1% property tax, $150 a month of insurance and PMI at 0.6%, that budget supports a home price of roughly $333,000. Using the lender's 28%-of-gross guideline instead, the same couple could spend $2,800 a month, which supports about $375,000, and some loan programs would stretch further still. The $40,000-plus difference is the room that keeps their retirement saving on track.

Key takeaway: Decide your comfortable monthly payment first, then work backward to the price, not the other way round.

Down payment: 20% or less?

Putting 20% down avoids PMI and lowers the payment, but waiting years to save it can mean paying rent and missing appreciation. Low-down-payment loans (conventional 3–5%, government-backed programs such as FHA in the US, or insured mortgages in Canada) can make sense when the payment fits comfortably and you still keep an emergency fund. Signs you're ready

  • Stable income and plans to stay at least five years
  • Credit score in a good tier (see how credit scores work)
  • Down payment and closing costs saved, with an emergency fund left over
  • High-interest debt paid off
