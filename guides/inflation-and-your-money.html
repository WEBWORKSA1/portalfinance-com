Inflation and your money

Inflation doesn't take money out of your account. It quietly shrinks what that money can buy. Here's how it works and what to do about it.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20267-minute read

What inflation is and how it's measured

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. Statistical agencies track it by pricing a fixed "basket" of goods and services every month. In the US, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Consumer Price Index (CPI); Statistics Canada, the UK's Office for National Statistics and other agencies publish equivalents. The Federal Reserve's preferred gauge is a related measure, the PCE price index.

Your personal inflation rate can differ from the headline number. If rent, child care or insurance take a big share of your budget and those prices rise fastest, your costs climb faster than the average.

Why central banks aim for about 2%

The Federal Reserve, the Bank of Canada, the Bank of England and the European Central Bank all target inflation around 2%. A little inflation gives room to cut interest rates in a downturn and avoids deflation, where falling prices encourage people to delay spending. When inflation runs hot, as it did in 2021–2022 when US CPI inflation peaked above 9% year over year, central banks raise interest rates to cool demand.

What inflation does to your money

Asset or debtEffect of higher inflation
Cash in low-interest accountsLoses purchasing power every year
Fixed-rate debt (e.g. a fixed mortgage)Gets cheaper in real terms as wages rise
Variable-rate debtUsually gets more expensive as rates rise
Existing bondsPrices fall when rates rise; new bonds pay more
StocksVolatile short-term; historically outpaced inflation over long periods
WagesOften lag, then catch up

At 3% a year, $10,000 left in a drawer buys about $5,500 worth of today's goods after 20 years. See your own numbers in the inflation calculator.

Key takeaway: The real return is what matters: your return minus inflation. A 4% savings rate during 5% inflation is a loss of purchasing power.

How to protect yourself

  1. Keep cash lean and earning. Hold your emergency fund and near-term goals in high-yield accounts, CDs or Treasury bills, and invest the rest for the long term.
  2. Own growth assets for long goals. Diversified stocks have historically beaten inflation over decades, though not every year.
  3. Consider inflation-linked bonds. US TIPS and I bonds adjust with CPI; other countries offer similar products.
  4. Lock in fixed rates on big debts when that suits your plans, and pay down variable-rate debt.
  5. Negotiate your pay. A raise below inflation is a pay cut in real terms.
  6. Update your budget yearly with the budget calculator as prices change.

Inflation in long-term plans

For retirement and other long goals, either use a real return (expected return minus inflation) or inflate your future costs. Our retirement calculator inflates your target income automatically, and the FIRE calculator works in today's dollars using a real return.
