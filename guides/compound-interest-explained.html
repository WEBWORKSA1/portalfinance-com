Compound interest explained: the math of getting rich slowly

Compounding is growth on top of growth. It's slow for years, then it isn't. Understanding it changes how you think about saving, fees and debt.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20267-minute read

Simple vs. compound interest

Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. $10,000 at 5% simple interest earns $500 every year: $15,000 after 10 years.

Compound interest is paid on the original amount plus all the interest already earned. The same $10,000 at 5% compounded yearly becomes $16,289 after 10 years, $26,533 after 20 and $43,219 after 30. The extra comes entirely from interest earning interest.

The formula

For a lump sum: FV = P × (1 + r/n)n×t, where P is the starting amount, r the annual rate, n how many times a year interest compounds and t the number of years. Regular contributions add many small lump sums, each compounding for less time. Our compound interest calculator handles both and shows the year-by-year table.

The rule of 72

Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate how many years it takes to double: 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years at 6%; 72 ÷ 9 = 8 years at 9%. It works in reverse for costs: at 3% inflation, prices double in about 24 years.

Why time beats amount

Consider two savers earning 7% a year, compounded monthly:

  • Early Ana invests $500 a month from 25 to 35, then stops. She contributes $60,000.
  • Later Leo invests $500 a month from 35 to 65. He contributes $180,000.

At 65, Ana has about $700,000; Leo has about $610,000. Ana put in a third as much and still ends ahead, because her money had three extra decades to double. You can't buy back time, which is why the best year to start is the current one.

Key takeaway: The three inputs are amount, rate and time. You control the first, can only partly influence the second, and the third is the most powerful.

When compounding works against you

Fees

A fee is a negative return that compounds too. Over 30 years, a portfolio earning 7% ends up about 25% smaller if it pays a 1% annual fee instead of nothing. That's why low-cost index funds matter.

Debt

Credit-card interest compounds monthly at rates often above 20%. A $5,000 balance at 24% paying only a small minimum can take well over a decade to clear and cost more than the original purchases. Paying down high-rate debt is a guaranteed return equal to its interest rate.

Inflation

Cash earning less than inflation is compounding backwards in real terms. See inflation and your money.

Putting it to work

  1. Automate a monthly investment, even a small one, and raise it with every pay rise.
  2. Use tax-advantaged accounts so taxes don't interrupt compounding.
  3. Reinvest dividends and leave the money alone through downturns.
  4. Keep costs low.
