Getting help

How to choose a financial advisor

A good advisor can be worth far more than they cost. A bad one can cost you for decades. Here's how to tell them apart before you sign.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20269-minute read

Do you need one?

If your finances are simple (one income, a workplace plan, no debt, an emergency fund), low-cost index funds and our calculators may be all you need. An advisor tends to earn their fee when you face complexity or a big decision: business ownership, stock options, an inheritance, divorce, retirement income planning, or when you know you'd panic-sell in a crash without someone to call.

Fiduciary vs. suitability

A fiduciary must act in your best interest and put your interests ahead of their own. In the US, registered investment advisers owe a fiduciary duty; broker-dealers follow Regulation Best Interest when making recommendations, which is a different standard. Ask directly: "Will you act as a fiduciary at all times, for all of my accounts, and put that in writing?"

A 1% annual fee on a growing portfolio adds up: use the compound interest calculator at 7% and 6% to see the long-run difference.

Credentials worth checking

  • CFP® (Certified Financial Planner): broad planning, an exam, experience requirements and a fiduciary commitment when giving financial advice.
  • CFA (Chartered Financial Analyst): deep investment analysis.
  • CPA/PFS: accountants with personal financial planning training, useful for tax-heavy situations.
  • Outside the US, look for the regulated equivalents, such as registered planners in Canada or FCA-authorised advisers in the UK.

Check their record in five minutes

  1. Search the advisor and firm on FINRA BrokerCheck and the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site.
  2. Verify a CFP® at the CFP Board.
  3. Read the firm's Form ADV Part 2 (US) for fees, conflicts and disciplinary history.
  4. Confirm your money will be held by an independent custodian, not the advisor personally.

Key takeaway: Fiduciary at all times, transparent fees, assets at an independent custodian and a clean record. Missing any of the four is a reason to keep looking.

10 questions to ask

  1. Are you a fiduciary at all times, in writing?
  2. How exactly are you paid, and what will I pay in total each year, in dollars?
  3. Do you earn commissions or bonuses on anything you recommend?
  4. Who holds my money?
  5. What are your credentials, and have you ever been disciplined?
  6. Who is your typical client?
  7. What services are included: tax, estate, insurance, retirement income?
  8. How often will we meet, and who will I actually work with?
  9. What's your investment approach, and what does it cost inside the funds?
  10. How do I leave, and are there exit fees?
