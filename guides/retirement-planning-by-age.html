Retirement planning by age: your 20s to your 60s

Each decade has a different job. Get the job for your decade right and the next one gets easier.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20269-minute read

Treat these as checkpoints, not verdicts. They assume saving about 15% of income from your mid-20s. If you're behind, the retirement calculator shows exactly how much more per month closes the gap.

In your 20s: build the habit

  • Contribute at least enough to get any employer match. It's an instant 50–100% return.
  • Clear high-interest debt and build an emergency fund.
  • Invest in low-cost, stock-heavy index funds; you have decades to ride out downturns.
  • Consider Roth-style accounts (Roth IRA, Roth 401(k), TFSA) while your tax rate is low.

In your 30s: protect and accelerate

  • Raise your saving rate with every raise; aim for 15% of gross income including any match.
  • Buy term life and disability insurance if anyone depends on your income.
  • Balance retirement with home buying and children's education, without stopping retirement contributions.

In your 40s: check the trajectory

  • Run a real projection. Peak earning years are the best time to close gaps.
  • Watch fees and consolidate old workplace accounts into low-cost options.
  • Start shifting gradually toward a more balanced stock and bond mix if your risk tolerance calls for it.

In your 50s: catch up and get specific

  • Use catch-up contributions; many retirement accounts allow higher limits from age 50. Check the current limits each year.
  • Estimate retirement spending line by line and decide when you'll claim government benefits.
  • Pay off the mortgage before retiring if it fits your plan, and protect the portfolio from a big loss just before you retire.

In your 60s: turn savings into income

  • Plan a withdrawal order across taxable, tax-deferred and tax-free accounts to manage lifetime taxes.
  • Hold one to three years of spending in cash and bonds so you don't sell stocks in a downturn.
  • Delaying government benefits generally increases your monthly payment; weigh that against health and longevity.
  • Revisit the 4% rule as a starting point for a sustainable withdrawal rate.

Key takeaway: Starting early matters most, saving rate matters next, and costs and taxes decide how much you keep.

Which account first?

  1. Workplace plan up to the full employer match
  2. Pay off debt above roughly 7–8% interest
  3. Tax-advantaged personal accounts (IRA/Roth IRA in the US; RRSP/TFSA in Canada; SIPP/ISA in the UK)
  4. Back to the workplace plan up to the limit
  5. Taxable brokerage account
