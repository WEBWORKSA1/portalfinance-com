Retirement

The 4% rule and FIRE, explained

Financial independence comes down to one ratio: what you spend versus what your investments can safely pay you. Here's where the famous 4% comes from and when to use something lower.

By the PortalFinance Editorial TeamUpdated November 12, 20268-minute read

Where the 4% rule comes from

In 1994, financial planner William Bengen tested historical US stock and bond returns to find the highest starting withdrawal rate that never ran out of money over 30 years. The answer was about 4% of the starting portfolio, increased each year with inflation. A 1998 study by three Trinity University professors, now known as the Trinity study, reached similar conclusions across different stock and bond mixes.

So with $1,000,000 you'd withdraw $40,000 in year one, then raise that amount with inflation each year regardless of market performance.

Your FIRE number

Flip the rule around: FIRE number = yearly spending ÷ withdrawal rate. At 4%, that's 25 times your annual spending. $40,000 a year needs $1 million; $60,000 needs $1.5 million. Our FIRE calculator adds how long it will take and your Coast FIRE number.

The rule's limits

  • It was built for 30 years. A 40-year-old retiree may need 50 or more years of income. Many early retirees plan with 3.25–3.5%.
  • It's based on US history. Other countries' markets produced lower safe rates in some periods.
  • Sequence risk. A crash in the first few years of retirement does far more damage than the same crash later.
  • Fees and taxes come out of the 4% too.

Lean, Fat, Barista and Coast FIRE

StyleWhat it means
Lean FIREIndependence on a minimal budget
Fat FIREIndependence with a generous budget, often 2–3× typical spending
Barista FIREPortfolio covers most costs; part-time work covers the rest and often health coverage
Coast FIREEnough invested that growth alone will reach your target by a normal retirement age

Why saving rate matters most

A higher saving rate works twice: you invest more, and you prove you can live on less, which lowers the target. Assuming a 5% real return, saving 25% of take-home pay suggests roughly 32 years to independence; 50% about 17 years; 65% about 10–11 years.

Key takeaway: Use 4% as a starting point, 3.25–3.5% for long early retirements, and stay willing to trim spending in bad market years.

Making withdrawals safer

  1. Flexible spending: cut withdrawals by 5–10% after a bad year.
  2. A cash and bond buffer covering one to three years of spending.
  3. Some income in the early years from part-time work, rental income or a side business.
  4. Delay government pensions to raise lifetime guaranteed income where that fits your situation.

Sources

  • Bengen, W. P. (1994). Determining withdrawal rates using historical data. Journal of Financial Planning.
  • Cooley, P. L., Hubbard, C. M., & Walz, D. T. (1998). Retirement savings: Choosing a withdrawal rate that is sustainable. AAII Journal.
