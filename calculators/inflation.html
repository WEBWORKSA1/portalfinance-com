How inflation compounds

Inflation compounds the same way interest does, only against you. Future cost is amount × (1 + inflation)years; purchasing power is amount ÷ (1 + inflation)years. At 3% a year, prices roughly double in 23 years, so $10,000 in a drawer buys about $5,500 worth of today's goods after 20 years.

Central banks such as the US Federal Reserve, the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England target about 2% inflation over time. Actual inflation swings well above and below target. Use a higher rate for long plans, or for costs that tend to rise faster than average, such as tuition and health care.

Protecting your money

  • Keep only your emergency fund and near-term goals in cash, and in accounts paying a competitive rate.
  • For long horizons, own assets that have historically outpaced inflation, such as diversified stocks.
  • Inflation-linked bonds (TIPS in the US, real return bonds elsewhere) adjust principal with inflation.

Inflation FAQ

What inflation rate should I use?

For long-term planning, 2.5–3% is a common middle estimate. For a stress test, try 4%.

What is the rule of 72?

Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate how many years it takes to double. At 3% inflation, prices double in about 24 years. This calculator shows the exact figure.
