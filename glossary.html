Finance glossary

The words that show up on statements, loan offers and investing apps, each defined in a sentence or two.

ABCDEFGHILMNPRSTVYZ

A

Amortization
Paying off a loan with regular fixed payments, where each payment covers that month's interest and reduces principal. See the loan calculator.
Annuity
A contract, usually with an insurer, that turns a lump sum into a stream of income, sometimes for life.
APR (annual percentage rate)
The yearly cost of borrowing, including the interest rate and most required fees. Use it to compare loans.
APY (annual percentage yield)
The yearly return on savings including compounding. Higher than the stated rate when interest compounds more than once a year.
Asset allocation
How a portfolio is divided among stocks, bonds, cash and other assets. It drives most of a portfolio's risk and return.

B

Balance transfer
Moving a credit-card balance to another card, often at a promotional 0% rate for a fixed period, usually for a fee of 3–5%.
Bear market
A decline of 20% or more from a recent market high.
Bond
A loan to a government or company that pays interest and returns the principal at maturity.
Brokerage account
A taxable investment account for buying stocks, funds and bonds.
Bull market
A sustained rise in market prices, often defined as a 20% gain from a recent low.

C

Capital gain
Profit from selling an asset for more than you paid. Often taxed differently from income.
CD (certificate of deposit)
A bank deposit that pays a fixed rate for a fixed term, with a penalty for early withdrawal. Called a GIC in Canada.
Closing costs
Fees paid when a home purchase or mortgage is finalised, typically 2–5% of the price.
Compound interest
Interest earned on both the original amount and previously earned interest. See our guide.
CPI (Consumer Price Index)
A measure of the average change in prices paid by consumers; the most-cited inflation gauge.
Credit utilization
Your card balances divided by your credit limits. Lower is better for your credit score.

D

Debt-to-income ratio (DTI)
Monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. Lenders use it to judge how much you can borrow.
Diversification
Spreading money across many investments so one failure doesn't sink the whole portfolio.
Dividend
A share of company profits paid to shareholders, usually in cash.
Dollar-cost averaging
Investing a fixed amount on a schedule regardless of price, which buys more shares when prices are low.
Down payment
The upfront portion of a home's price paid in cash; the rest is financed with a mortgage.

E

Emergency fund
Cash set aside for unexpected, necessary expenses, typically three to six months of essentials.
Equity
Ownership value: a home's value minus the mortgage, or shares of stock in a company.
Escrow
An account a lender uses to collect and pay property taxes and insurance on your behalf.
ETF (exchange-traded fund)
A fund that trades on an exchange like a stock; many track an index at low cost.
Expense ratio
A fund's yearly fee as a percentage of your investment.

F

Fiduciary
A professional legally obliged to act in your best interest.
FIRE
Financial Independence, Retire Early: saving aggressively until investments can cover your spending. See the FIRE calculator.
Fixed rate
An interest rate that stays the same for the life of a loan or term.
Front-end ratio
Housing costs divided by gross income; a common guideline is 28% or less.

G

Gross income
Income before taxes and deductions.
Growth stock
A company expected to grow earnings faster than average, often paying little or no dividend.

H

HELOC
Home equity line of credit: a revolving loan secured by your home's equity.
High-yield savings account
A deposit account paying a higher rate than a standard savings account, often at online banks.

I

Index fund
A fund that holds all the securities in a market index to match its return at low cost.
Inflation
The general rise in prices, which reduces what money can buy. See the inflation calculator.
Interest rate
The cost of borrowing, or the reward for saving, as a percentage per year.
IRA
Individual Retirement Account (US): a tax-advantaged personal retirement account, in traditional or Roth form.

L

Liquidity
How quickly an asset can be turned into cash without losing value.
Loan-to-value (LTV)
The loan amount divided by the property's value. Lower LTV usually means better terms.

M

Market capitalization
A company's share price times the number of shares: its total stock market value.
Minimum payment
The smallest amount due on a debt each month to avoid a late fee; paying only this keeps you in debt longer.
Money market fund
A low-risk fund holding short-term debt, used for cash management.
Mortgage
A loan used to buy property, secured by that property. See the mortgage calculator.
Mutual fund
A pooled investment bought from the fund company at the end-of-day price.

N

Net income
Take-home pay after taxes and deductions.
Net worth
Everything you own minus everything you owe.

P

P/E ratio
Share price divided by earnings per share; a rough gauge of how expensive a stock is.
PMI (private mortgage insurance)
Insurance that protects the lender, usually required on conventional loans with less than 20% down.
Portfolio
The full collection of your investments.
Prepayment penalty
A fee some lenders charge for paying off a loan early.
Principal
The original amount borrowed or invested, not including interest.

R

Rebalancing
Selling some of what's grown and buying what's lagged to return to your target mix.
Recession
A significant, widespread decline in economic activity lasting more than a few months.
Refinancing
Replacing a loan with a new one, usually to get a lower rate or different term.
Roth account
A retirement account funded with after-tax money where qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
RRSP / TFSA
Canada's main tax-advantaged accounts: RRSP contributions are deductible; TFSA growth and withdrawals are tax-free.
Rule of 72
72 divided by the annual rate estimates how many years it takes money (or prices) to double.

S

Safe withdrawal rate
The share of a portfolio you can spend each year with a low chance of running out; 4% is the classic figure.
Secured card
A credit card backed by a cash deposit, used to build or rebuild credit.
Sequence-of-returns risk
The danger that poor returns early in retirement deplete savings faster than the same returns later.
Stock
A share of ownership in a company.

T

Target-date fund
A fund that automatically shifts from stocks to bonds as a set retirement year approaches.
Tax-advantaged account
An account such as a 401(k), IRA, RRSP, TFSA or ISA that reduces or defers taxes on savings.
Term life insurance
Life cover for a fixed period at a low cost, with no investment component.
Treasury bill
Short-term US government debt sold at a discount and repaid at face value.

V

Variable rate
An interest rate that changes with a benchmark rate.
Volatility
How much and how quickly an investment's price moves up and down.

Y

Yield
The income an investment produces, such as interest or dividends, as a percentage of its price.
Yield curve
A chart of interest rates across bond maturities; an inverted curve has often preceded recessions.

Z

Zero-based budget
A budget where every dollar of income is assigned a job until nothing is left unplanned. See our budgeting guide.
